Why Do Some Mobile Games Work in Portrait Mode While Others Use Landscape?

How available space and intended interaction shape orientation choices

Mobile interfaces can be designed around either a taller or wider working area depending on how information and controls need to be arranged. Design Considerations That Drive the Choice

One-handed use

Portrait is generally better for one-handed operation — the thumb reaches most of the screen comfortably, making quick interactions more natural without repositioning the grip.

Wide game areas

Landscape suits games where horizontal extent matters — side-scrollers, racing games, strategy views with a wide map — because the wider frame shows more content without shrinking it.

Information hierarchy

Portrait suits content organized in vertical columns — news feeds, card lists, turn-based card games — where scrolling down reveals more of the same type of content.

Control placement

Landscape allows controls on both sides simultaneously — left hand for movement, right hand for actions — which suits games designed for two-handed input on a wider surface.

Immersive cinematic look

Landscape matches the widescreen aspect ratio familiar from cinema and television, which may suit games aiming for an immersive visual experience.

Reading and information density

Portrait can display more text lines before requiring scrolling. Games with significant text — RPG dialogue, card descriptions — may favor portrait for reading comfort.

Why games often lock to one orientation

Rather than supporting both orientations, many games lock to one. This is because designing an interface that works well in both portrait and landscape requires essentially building two different layouts. Games typically choose the orientation that best suits their core interaction and stick to it, rather than creating a responsive system that handles both satisfactorily. When orientation is locked, rotating the device has no effect on the game's display.
